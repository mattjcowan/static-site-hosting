Your first night out

Stargazing 101

You do not need a telescope to start. You need twenty minutes, a chair, and a few tricks every observer learns the hard way.

1 · Your eyes

Twenty minutes of darkness

Your eyes need 20–30 minutes in the dark to reach full sensitivity. One glance at a white screen resets the clock, which is what red light is for: press Red light above and this page turns red.

Start this when you step outside. Put the phone away; come back when it is full.

2 · Binoculars first

Your first instrument

A pair of 10×50 binoculars (10 times magnification, 50 mm lenses) shows craters on the Moon, Jupiter's moons, the Orion Nebula and hundreds of star clusters. They are also the best way to learn the sky, because they show wide fields you can match to a chart.

When you do buy a telescope, a 6–8 inch Dobsonian gives the most light for the money.

3 · How dark is your sky?

The Bortle scale

Astronomers rate sky darkness from 1 (the darkest skies on Earth) to 9 (a city centre). Most suburbs sit around 5–6, where the Milky Way is gone but the Moon, planets and bright clusters are all still there.

4 · Finding your way

Star-hopping

  1. Start from a star you know, like Vega or Betelgeuse.
  2. Use shapes as signposts: "two belt-lengths below Orion's belt".
  3. Your fist at arm's length covers about 10° of sky: a ruler you always have with you.
  4. Sketch what you see. It trains your eye faster than anything else.
5 · Before you go

A short checklist

  • Check the Moon tonight: a bright Moon hides faint things.
  • Dress one layer warmer than seems reasonable. Standing still is cold.
  • A red light, a chair, and a hot drink.
  • Tell someone where you are going if you drive somewhere dark.
